# Training on noisy report-derived labels, evaluated against expert image reads (2022–2026 evidence)

Scope: RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection (2026). ~4400 knee MRI studies, 12 findings, soft LLM-extracted report labels for training, 58 expert image-read studies, hidden test = expert image reads, metric = macro ROC-AUC (ranking only), 2x T4 / ~60 GPU-hours. Already working: soft bootstrapping (0.5 report + 0.5 OOF of a different architecture). Already saturated: more pseudo-label rounds; better report extraction barely moved LB; label-noise-agnostic ensembling gives a little.

Conventions in these notes: "Cited Findings" are things I read in the source (PDF text read directly unless flagged "per page summary"); "Inferences" are mine, including my own arithmetic. Compute cost is given as a multiple of one ordinary training run (the baseline cost on the T4s is not known to me).

Two facts that frame everything below:
1. Under class-conditional label noise (flip rates depend only on the true class), the AUC measured on noisy labels is an affine function of the clean AUC, so the AUC-optimal ranking is unchanged; noise only costs sample efficiency. What hurts ranking against expert reads is therefore instance-dependent noise (reporting depends on site, co-occurring findings, clinical context), not the overall omission rate. See Q1.
2. Almost every "noisy-label" paper in medical imaging scores against labels that are themselves noisy or against a noisy-train/clean-test split with thousands of clean items. Nothing I found validates a method with a clean set as small as 58. See Q3.

## Q1. Which label-noise-robust losses/methods have strong medical evidence (co-teaching, DivideMix, ELR, GCE/SCE, label smoothing, transition matrices, meta-learning with a clean set), and what did they gain on report-labelled chest X-ray?

### Takeaway
On real, report-derived noise the evidence is that generic noise-robust methods (co-teaching, DivideMix, MentorNet, forward correction, ELR) are about equal to plain BCE/CE or worse, and sample-selection methods are actively harmful under class imbalance. The few things that beat the baseline on clean test sets are multi-label-specific: EMA "non-volatile" memory of the model's own logits with a class-prior logit shift (NVUM: +3 to +5 AUC points over its own plain-BCE ablation baseline, +2 to +3 over the strongest prior CXR classifier), explicit noise priors measured on a small re-read subset (+0.02 AUC on some labels), and ensembling. Meta-learning with a clean set has only tiny, unreplicated medical evidence, and none at n=58.

### Cited Findings
- A 2024 scoping review of 60 medical noisy-label papers (2016–2023) recommends noise-robust loss functions, weighting and curriculum learning as practical starting points for medical researchers. — [Scoping review, arXiv 2403.13111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13111) (per page summary)
- LNMBench (Dec 2025, 10 methods, 7 datasets, 6 modalities): "performance of existing LNL methods degrades substantially under high and real-world noise." Real-world noisy sets include CheXpert, whose official test set was read by 8 radiologists; the CheXpert subset is single-label 5-class with uncertain=negative, scored by accuracy. Last-5-epoch accuracy on CheXpert: CE 37.08, SCE 40.46, CDR 37.96, Co-teaching 35.33, T-Revision 33.57, DISC 31.39, DivideMix 21.17. Best-epoch (oracle) accuracy: CE 43.07, SCE 48.96, CDR 42.33, Co-teaching 37.22, DivideMix 33.58. Their text: "only SCE and CDR achieve slightly better performance than CE on CheXpert. Sample selection based methods perform poorly." — [LNMBench, arXiv 2512.09315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09315) (Table 4, read from PDF text)
- Same paper, mechanism: in imbalanced data, clean minority-class samples have higher early loss than noisy ones, so small-loss selection discards them; "their performance on imbalanced datasets can be even worse than that of standard CE"; pure sample-selection methods also assume the noise rate is known. Their remedy MedSSL (label smoothing in warm-up + regulariser + class-specific selection thresholds) lifts DivideMix 21.17 to 35.01 and DISC 31.39 to 40.71 (last-epoch accuracy), i.e. only up to about the level of plain SCE. — [LNMBench, arXiv 2512.09315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09315)
- NVUM (MICCAI 2022): multi-label CXR trained on noisy NIH labels, tested on clean OpenI/PadChest (radiologist-labelled). Mean AUC (OpenI / PadChest): CheXNet-style CheXNet baseline 83.69 / 83.29; strongest prior CXR classifier (Hermoza et al.) 86.01 / 83.25; DivideMix 70.92 / 76.18; NVUM 88.65 / 85.55. Trained on CheXpert instead: baseline 76.64 / 78.12, DivideMix 69.36 / 65.72, NVUM 77.99 / 80.96. Authors: noisy multi-class small-loss methods are "insufficient" for noisy multi-label imbalanced data. Training took 15 h on NIH (RTX 2080 Ti), one model. — [NVUM, arXiv 2103.04053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04053) (Tables 1–2, read from PDF text)
- NVUM mechanism and ablation: running average (beta=0.9) of the model's own logits stored per sample ("non-volatile" memory) used as a regularisation target (ELR-like), plus adding log class-prior to the logits. Ablation on NIH→OpenI/PadChest: plain BCE 82.91 / 82.27; adding the prior to BCE or to the regulariser gives about +2 on both; full NVUM 88.17 / 85.49 (+5.3 / +3.2 over plain BCE). Volatile memory (beta=0.1) was "significantly worse"; larger beta (0.5 to 0.9) improved monotonically. — [NVUM, arXiv 2103.04053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04053) (Fig. 2, text)
- NVUM Table 3 (NIH subset of 1,962 images re-read by at least 3 radiologists; pneumothorax AUC / mass-nodule AUC): BCE 87.0 / 84.3; forward correction 80.8 / 84.8; MentorNet 86.6 / 83.7; Decoupling 80.1 / 84.3; Co-teaching 87.3 / 82.0; ELR 87.1 / 83.2; Xue et al. (two-model co-training) 89.1 / 84.6; NVUM 88.9 / 85.5. So ELR and co-teaching are indistinguishable from BCE here. The authors call this subset less reliable than OpenI/PadChest. — [NVUM, arXiv 2103.04053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04053)
- BoMD (ICCV 2023) is a further noisy-multi-label CXR method evaluated with noisy NIH/CheXpert training and clean OpenI/PadChest test sets; I could not retrieve its numbers (HTTP 403). — [BoMD, ICCV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/Chen_BoMD_Bag_of_Multi-label_Descriptors_for_Noisy_Chest_X-ray_Classification_ICCV_2023_paper.html) (search snippet only)
- ELR on natural-image real noise (Clothing1M, ResNet-50): CE 69.10, forward 69.84, GCE 69.75, SL 71.02, Joint-Optim 72.16, ELR 72.87, DivideMix 74.76, ELR+ 74.81. Mechanism: EMA of the model's predictions as per-sample targets plus a regulariser, no sample filtering. — [ELR, arXiv 2007.00151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00151) (Table 3)
- Self-Adaptive Training: training targets are an EMA of all historical model predictions plus confidence re-weighting; "without incurring extra computational cost". — [Self-Adaptive Training, arXiv 2002.10319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10319) (abstract via search; paper pdf listed)
- Measured noise on report labels: on 689 ChestX-ray14 images re-read by 4 radiologists, NLP labels had sensitivity of only 0.30 (effusion), 0.34 (cardiomegaly), 0.13 (consolidation), 0.22 (atelectasis), 0.36 (mass) and specificity 0.95–0.99 — i.e. noise is overwhelmingly missed positives. They put these measured priors into the loss as a regulariser (lambda 0.1, sensitivity/specificity from a calibration split of the re-read set) and score on radiologist consensus labels; ablation gave e.g. consolidation +0.024 AUC (p=0.037); full system average AUC 0.880 over 17 abnormalities. — [Gündel et al., arXiv 2104.05261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05261) (numbers per ar5iv page summary; sensitivities average to 0.271 as the abstract-level summary also stated)
- Theory that matters here: Menon et al. show that under class-conditional corruption (and PU/censoring, which is the one-sided case) AUC_corrupted(s) = (1 − α − β)·AUC_clean(s) + (α + β)/2, "one can optimise balanced error and AUC without knowledge of the corruption process parameters", and the corrupted-data class-probability estimator has good clean AUC; the price is a regret-bound factor 1/(1 − α − β). — [Menon et al., ICML 2015](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/menon15.html) ([paper PDF, eq. 3, 8, Prop. 2, Cor. 3](https://users.cecs.anu.edu.au/~akmenon/papers/corrupted-labels/corrupted-labels-paper.pdf), read from PDF text)
- Gold Loss Correction (trusted data): estimate the corruption matrix by averaging an untrusted-data classifier's predictions over trusted examples of each class, apply correction only to untrusted samples; experiments use 0.1%–25% trusted data (typically 1–10%) on CIFAR/MNIST/SVHN/SST/IMDB/Twitter; gains largest at moderate to severe noise. — [Hendrycks et al., NeurIPS 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05300) (read from PDF text)
- Meta-learning with a small clean set in medicine: ROP retinal images, 622 consensus images of which 200 = clean meta-data and 300 = test, 1,447 no-consensus images as noisy training; ResNet-50 pretrained. Test accuracy: CE 86.3, CE with DR pretraining 90.3, meta soft-label method 91.4 (+1.1 over the pretrained CE; no significance test reported). — [Algan & Ulusoy, arXiv 2010.06939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06939) (Table I, read from PDF text; method family: [MSLG, arXiv 2007.05836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05836))
- Learning to Reweight examples (ICML 2018) is the original "small clean validation set drives per-sample weights via gradient directions" method; it needs a bi-level step per iteration. — [L2RW, arXiv 1803.09050](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09050) (abstract only; overhead not stated on the page)
- Two cheap multi-label-specific alternatives aimed at missing positives: Asymmetric Loss (down-weights and hard-thresholds easy negatives and "discarding possibly mislabeled samples", no extra training cost) and Large-Loss rejection/correction for partially labelled multi-label data (treat unobserved as negative, then reject or correct the highest-loss negatives; memorisation effect also occurs in multi-label). On OpenImages V3 with real partial labels mAP is naive-assume-negative 82.0 vs LL-R 82.5 / LL-Ct 82.6 / LL-Cp 82.5. — [ASL, arXiv 2009.14119](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14119); [Large Loss Matters, CVPR 2022, arXiv 2206.03740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03740) (Table 3, read from PDF text; natural images, not medical)
- The report-vs-image gap is category-dependent in MRI: head MRI (126,556 exams, BioBERT report labeller, 950 exams with reference-standard image labels): AUC on report labels vs on image labels dropped 0.991→0.977 for abnormal/normal, but atrophy 0.970→0.937, encephalomalacia 0.977→0.922, vascular 0.956→0.883. — [Wood et al., Eur Radiol 2021, PMC8660736](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8660736) (per page summary; I could not retrieve the abstract verbatim)

### Inferences
- Class-conditional omission alone is not why your ranking is capped: the "reports omit findings" pattern (expert prevalence 0.40 vs report 0.23 for lateral meniscus) is one-sided noise. If it were class-conditional, my reading of Menon eq. (3)/(8): ρ+ = 1 − 0.23/0.40 = 0.425, β = π·ρ+/(1 − π_corr) = 0.17/0.77 ≈ 0.22, α = 0, so AUC_report = 0.78·AUC_expert + 0.11 and the optimal ranking is identical. This is my arithmetic, not a published number, and assumes hidden positives are missed at the same rate whatever the image looks like. The evidence that site identity predicts report labels better than expert labels (0.64 vs 0.59) says that assumption fails, so the gap you can still close is the instance-dependent part (site/reporter style, co-occurrence, "reportability" of a finding), plus variance.
- Therefore prefer methods that (a) reduce variance/overfitting to the noisy targets (EMA targets, soft targets, ensembling) or (b) model instance-dependent noise (site-specific terms, co-occurrence), and do not spend compute on transition-matrix estimation, prevalence correction or sample-selection methods. Correcting a global flip rate cannot change a ranking.
- Co-teaching/DivideMix/MentorNet are 2x cost (two networks) and, in the only head-to-head with clean radiologist test sets that I found, they are at or below BCE. Rank them last. SCE/CDR are free to try but the supporting evidence is single-label accuracy on a 5-class subset, so it is weak support for a sigmoid multi-label AUC task.
- ELR/NVUM-style EMA targets are the same family as your soft bootstrapping (targets = mix of report label and model predictions). The evidence supports stacking them (self-EMA target + cross-architecture OOF target), not replacing one with the other, and supports the class-prior logit shift during training (NVUM ablation +2) which costs nothing. It is plausible the large gain you saw from OOF bootstrapping is the same mechanism NVUM reports, which also explains why more rounds saturate.
- Meta-learning (L2RW/MWN/MLC/MSLG) needs a clean set large enough to estimate a stable bilevel gradient per label; the only medical datapoint uses 200 clean images for a 3-class task and gains +1.1 accuracy points. With 58 studies spread over 12 findings (some with single-digit positives) and a 2–3x cost, this is not worth the T4 hours.

### Gaps
- No paper I found evaluates ELR, GCE, SCE or label-smoothing variants on multi-label report-labelled MRI against expert image reads. All numbers above are chest X-ray (or retina/natural images).
- LNMBench's CheXpert result is single-label accuracy on a filtered subset, not multi-label AUC.
- BoMD's quantitative results and any ELR-vs-NVUM-vs-bootstrapping head-to-head on multi-label data could not be retrieved or do not exist in what I found.
- MWN (Meta-Weight-Net) and MLC (Meta Label Correction) original numbers were not retrieved; only the L2RW abstract and a medical MSLG-style paper were read.

## Q2. Knowledge distillation / self-training loops (noisy student, mean teacher, temporal ensembling) for report-label settings: how many rounds, which teacher?

### Takeaway
Published self-training evidence says gains are front-loaded: 2–3 generations at most, with each later round smaller, soft targets beat hard ones, and the student must be noised and at least as large as the teacher. Medical temporal-ensembling evidence adds that plain temporal ensembling can hurt and only helps when gated by prediction uncertainty. Your saturation after a few rounds matches this, and the likely residual problem is that the teacher inherits the site/reporter bias, which more rounds cannot remove.

### Cited Findings
- Noisy Student (ImageNet): best model came from three iterations; second iteration brought 87.6% → 88.1%, third (with a larger unlabeled:labeled batch ratio) reached 88.4%. Findings listed by the authors: soft pseudo labels work better than hard ones for out-of-domain data in some cases; student should be larger than the teacher; noise (dropout, stochastic depth, RandAugment) on the student is important; joint training on labelled + unlabelled beats pretrain-then-finetune; training the student from scratch is sometimes better than initialising from the teacher. — [Noisy Student, arXiv 1911.04252](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04252) (Sec. 4.2, Table 7, findings list, read from PDF text)
- Born-Again Networks: sequential self-distillation students beat their teachers, but an ensemble of all generations does not beat an ensemble of the same number of independently trained models. — [Born-Again Networks, arXiv 1805.04770](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04770) (per search-result summary)
- Uncertainty-aware temporal ensembling in CXR (NIH + CheXpert + private data): joint-training baseline 0.8200 mean AUC on the NIH test set; plain temporal ensembling without uncertainty gating lowered the AUC (0.8320 → 0.8291); uncertainty-aware temporal ensembling (UTE) alone reached 0.8302; the full method (task weighting + task-specific adversarial site/domain training + UTE) reached 0.8349, which the authors describe as a 1.2-point gain in average AUC over simple joint training (their text; the 0.8200 vs 0.8349 figures differ by 1.5); UTE weight 30 and adversarial weight 0.03 were the best settings (adversarial weight 0.3 hurt). Caveat: NIH test labels are themselves report-derived. — [Luo et al., TMI 2020, arXiv 2006.03796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03796) (Table VI and text, read from PDF text)
- CheXpert "U-SelfTrained": train a model ignoring uncertain labels, then re-label the uncertain ones with its predicted probabilities (never replacing 0/1), retrain. On the 200-study radiologist-consensus validation set it was best for consolidation but not uniformly better than U-Ones/U-Zeros/U-MultiClass (AUC 0.833, 0.831, 0.939, 0.935, 0.932 for atelectasis…effusion vs U-Ignore 0.818, 0.828, 0.938, 0.934, 0.928). — [CheXpert, arXiv 1901.07031](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07031) (Table 3, read from PDF text; I reassigned the garbled row order using the paper's in-text numbers)
- Label cleaning by out-of-fold predictions in a Kaggle report-free but site-biased setting: PANDA 1st place (Team PND) trained 5-fold base models, predicted hold-out folds, removed training samples whose label disagreed with the OOF prediction using different thresholds for the two data providers (README: a variant that "remove[s] 20% Radboud labels"), then retrained and ensembled models trained on differently cleaned sets. The provider-specific thresholds are my reading of the cleaned-file name (`..._rad_13_08_ka_15_10.csv`) and the README line "remove 20% Radboud labels". — [PANDA 1st place repo](https://github.com/kentaroy47/kaggle-panda-1st-place-solution) (README; I could not retrieve the Kaggle write-up text, so the effect of cleaning on CV/LB is not known to me)
- In labeler-quality experiments, a labeler trained to predict image-based labels (VisualCheXbert, supervised by a DenseNet's predicted image labels as proxy) beat CheXpert/CheXbert labelers in agreement with radiologists reading images (F1 +0.14 average) and models trained on its labels scored higher (reported weighted AUROC 0.87 vs 0.83). — [VisualCheXbert, arXiv 2102.11467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11467); [Effect of report labeler quality, arXiv 2104.00793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00793) (numbers per ar5iv page summary)
- Pham et al. (CheXpert): the biggest single jump came from ensembling. Single best model U-Ones+conditional training+label-smoothing 0.894 mean AUC on the validation set; ensemble of six models 0.940; template-matching preprocessing (+0.006) and test-time augmentation (+0.003) were marginal; the authors attribute the main gain to conditional training and label smoothing. — [Pham et al., arXiv 1911.06475](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06475) (text and Table 1, read from PDF text)

### Inferences
- How many rounds: 1–2 beyond the first teacher is where published gains are; the third was +0.3 and confounded with a hyperparameter change. You have already seen saturation, which is consistent. Further rounds only make sense if something new enters the loop (a different architecture/input resolution, site-corrected targets, or the expert signal).
- Teacher choice: cross-architecture OOF teachers (your setup) are the right call; the student should be noised and as large or larger; try initialising students from scratch rather than from the teacher (Noisy Student finding) to avoid inheriting the teacher's fit to noisy labels. Teacher OOF predictions need to be strictly out-of-fold, otherwise the target is a memorised copy of the report label.
- Gate pseudo-label weight by confidence/uncertainty rather than applying a uniform mix: Luo's ablation shows ungated temporal ensembling lost 0.3 AUC points while gated gained. A cheap implementation is to make the blend weight per study a function of OOF entropy or disagreement between two teachers.
- Because the teacher is trained on site-biased labels, its OOF predictions carry the same bias (confirmation bias). PANDA's winners addressed this by treating the two data sources differently when cleaning; the analogue here is applying a site-specific rule when blending/cleaning (Q4).
- Ensembling of independently trained models is the best-evidenced lever in the CXR literature (+4.6 points in Pham, versus +1.4 for label smoothing) and is cheap relative to bilevel/two-network schemes, but Born-Again results warn that "ensembles of generations" are weaker than ensembles of independent runs, so diversify (architecture, seed, resolution, label encoding) rather than adding generations.

### Gaps
- No study I found tests self-training rounds against expert image-read labels for report-labelled MRI; Noisy Student numbers are ImageNet.
- The PANDA write-up with CV/LB effects of the OOF cleaning could not be retrieved (page returned only the title).
- Mean Teacher / temporal ensembling (Tarvainen & Valpola; Laine & Aila) original papers were not read; only the CXR use in Luo et al.

## Q3. How to use a tiny expert set (58 studies) without overfitting: calibration, per-label weighting, prior correction, validation-only use; risks.

### Takeaway
Treat the 58 studies as a sanity/selection signal with explicit uncertainty, not as a tuning surface: at n=58 the standard error of a per-label AUC is 0.05–0.11 and about 0.024 for the macro average (my calculation), so only large, consistent, cross-label effects are detectable. The literature that did learn a report-to-image mapping used 200–689 re-read images and still saw per-label winner flips that were not significant. If the set is used for anything beyond validation, restrict to 1–2 shrunk parameters per label and use nested cross-validation; prior/prevalence correction is irrelevant to ROC-AUC.

### Cited Findings
- CheXpert validated its uncertainty-handling choices on 200 studies read by a 3-radiologist image consensus; most per-label differences between approaches were not significant (only atelectasis U-Ones 0.858 vs U-Zeros 0.811, p=0.03, and cardiomegaly U-MultiClass 0.854 vs U-Ignore 0.828, p<0.01 were), yet the final model chose a different approach per label (U-Ones for atelectasis and edema, U-MultiClass for cardiomegaly and effusion, U-SelfTrained for consolidation) based on that 200-study set. — [CheXpert, arXiv 1901.07031](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07031) (read from PDF text)
- Learning a report-to-image mapping with a small image-labelled set: VisualCheXbert's baseline fits an L1-regularised logistic regression from the one-hot report labels of all conditions to each image label, trained on the 500-study CheXpert test set (5-radiologist image majority). Weighted F1 rose from 0.56 (naive zero/one mapping) to 0.70; the fitted odds ratios show cross-label structure (e.g. a positive effusion report multiplies the odds of lung opacity by 4.4; atelectasis-uncertain reports raise lung-opacity odds 2.9x). — [VisualCheXbert, arXiv 2102.11467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11467) (Table 4, Sec. 4.3, read from PDF text)
- Same paper, why reports and images disagree: radiologists labelling reports vs images show low agreement (weighted-average F1 between 0.49 and 0.58 depending on pessimistic/optimistic mapping), with disagreements explained by clinical context, prior imaging, what the referrer asked for, and conditions not being commented on when clinically irrelevant (e.g. atelectasis rarely reported in ICU patients with support devices). — [VisualCheXbert, arXiv 2102.11467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11467)
- Noise priors from a small re-read: sensitivity/specificity of NLP labels measured on a calibration split of a 689-image radiologist re-read, used as a loss regulariser (lambda 0.1), improved consolidation AUC by +0.024 (p=0.037). — [Gündel et al., arXiv 2104.05261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05261) (per ar5iv page summary)
- Mixing clean and weak labels beat sequential fine-tuning in an MRI study: glioma change detection, weak labels from reports increased the dataset more than 3-fold; VGG AUC 0.75 → 0.82 (p=0.04), and "mixed training from scratch led to higher performance than fine-tuning or feature extraction". — [Di Noto et al., arXiv 2210.09698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.09698) (abstract, read from PDF text)
- GLC and L2RW need a clean fraction of 1–10% (GLC) or an unspecified small validation set (L2RW) of a dataset of tens of thousands; see Q1. 58 of 4400 is 1.3% but spread over 12 labels. — [GLC, arXiv 1802.05300](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05300)
- Joint training on labelled and unlabelled data beat pretrain-then-fine-tune in Noisy Student (finding 6). — [Noisy Student, arXiv 1911.04252](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04252)

### Inferences
- Precision of the 58-study set (my calculation, Hanley–McNeil standard error for AUC with n=58): prevalence 0.40 (about 23 positives) SE 0.063 at AUC 0.80 and 0.046 at AUC 0.90; prevalence 0.20 (12 positives) SE 0.081 at 0.80; prevalence 0.10 (6 positives) SE 0.112 at 0.80. Macro-AUC SE about 0.024 if the 12 labels were independent with a plausible spread of prevalences (assumed values 0.10–0.40). A rule: only trust a change if it improves the macro score and at least about 9 of 12 labels, and confirm with the Kaggle LB; paired bootstrap/DeLong over the 58 for model-vs-model comparisons.
- Calibration and prevalence/prior correction do not change per-label ROC-AUC (monotone transformations), so do not spend any of the 58 on them. They matter only inside blends (blend on logit or rank scale, not raw probability) and if scores are pooled across sites, where site-specific offsets learned from report prevalence could distort the pooled ranking; check pooled vs within-site AUC on the 58.
- Per-label weighting: the expert-vs-report prevalence gap is a usable, cheap statistic to estimate "hidden-positive rate" per label (for lateral meniscus (0.40 − 0.23)/(1 − 0.23) ≈ 22% of report-negatives are hidden positives; my arithmetic from the numbers in the task statement). Estimating that gap needs only prevalence on 58 studies (SE about 0.065 for a 0.4 prevalence), much less demanding than estimating a mapping. It can set a per-label rejection/correction budget (Q5) or per-label blend weight, with shrinkage toward the pooled average gap.
- A VisualCheXbert-style stacker (expert label ~ regularised function of the 12 report probabilities, 12 OOF image probabilities, site) is the best-evidenced way to use cross-label structure, but the published fit used 500 studies. With 58, fit only the own-label coefficient pair per label (two parameters), pool across labels with a shared prior (ridge toward a global 0.5/0.5 blend), and evaluate with leave-one-out or repeated 5-fold on the 58. Do not fit the full 24-input model.
- Mixed training (expert studies included in the training mix at high weight) beats sequential fine-tuning in two independent papers, but it consumes the validation signal. Possible compromise: use frozen embeddings/OOF predictions and fit the small stacker (cheap, nested CV) instead of retraining backbones with the 58.
- Risks: per-label model selection on 58 (CheXpert-style winner flips are mostly noise); repeated peeking inflates the apparent gain (each extra comparison on the same 58 consumes statistical power); expert labels treat borderline as negative while reports encode borderline as uncertain, so a stacker may learn to push borderline reports toward 0 on a handful of cases.

### Gaps
- No paper tests any clean-set method with fewer than about 200 clean items in a medical setting; the minimum I found is 200 (ROP meta-data).
- The Hanley–McNeil SEs above are my computation under assumed prevalences; the real per-label expert prevalences/positive counts in the 58 were not available to me.
- No evidence on "borderline negative" expert conventions specifically.

## Q4. Domain/site shortcut mitigation: site-adversarial training, group DRO, site-balanced sampling, removing site-specific label bias; evidence in medical imaging.

### Takeaway
Evidence that formal bias-mitigation algorithms (group DRO, adversarial) beat ERM in medical imaging is negative at benchmark scale (MEDFAIR), but cheap resampling to break the site-label correlation worked strongly in a controlled CheXpert shortcut study and small-weight task-specific adversarial site training gave a sub-1-point AUC gain in a multi-dataset CXR study. None of these target the thing you measured (site-specific label bias, not site-visible image bias), for which the most direct published tool is a learned annotator/site confusion model; a caution is that site also carries some true signal (0.59 AUC on expert labels).

### Cited Findings
- MEDFAIR (ICLR 2023): 11 bias-mitigation algorithms (including group DRO, adversarial, resampling) x 10 datasets x 3 model-selection criteria: "state-of-the-art bias mitigation algorithms do not significantly improve fairness outcomes over ERM in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings"; model-selection criterion "can have a significant impact". — [MEDFAIR, arXiv 2210.01725](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725) (abstract, per page summary)
- Controlled shortcut study on CheXpert (pleural effusion with an induced sex–disease correlation), AUROC for the disease on validation / inverted-correlation / balanced test distributions: baseline 98.5 / 45.8 / 80.5; plain rebalancing (resampling) – / 79.8 / 90.0; MINE-based dependence minimisation 93.8 / 87.7 / 92.4; distance-correlation 98.3 / 53.4 / 83.0; adversarial classifier 95.5 / 67.4 / 84.9. Authors: "Dataset rebalancing was surprisingly effective", dCor and adversarial classifier "showed lower performance on the medical task, indicating limited robustness across domains", MINE trains longer. — [Müller et al., arXiv 2407.18792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18792) (Table 5 and discussion, read from PDF text)
- Task-specific adversarial domain training (per-disease adversarial alignment of NIH/CheXpert features) on top of task weighting reached mean AUC 0.8306 on the NIH test set and gained about 0.6 points on the shared categories; mean AUC was stable for adversarial weight 0 to 0.1, large drop at 0.3 where "the adversarial loss dominates the training". Labels are report-derived on both datasets. — [Luo et al., arXiv 2006.03796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03796) (text of Sec. IV-F, read from PDF text)
- Learning per-annotator confusion matrices jointly with the classifier, with a trace regulariser to make them identifiable, "capable of estimating the skills of annotators even with a single label available per image"; reported on par with or better than state of the art on simulated and real (including medical) labels. A site can be treated as an "annotator" with its own reporting style. — [Tanno et al., CVPR 2019, arXiv 1902.03680](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.03680) (abstract, read from PDF text)
- Shortcut testing in clinical models (Google/Brown et al., Nat Commun 2023): a multitask-learning test for whether a sensitive/site attribute is encoded and how that correlates with fairness metrics; demonstrated for radiology and dermatology. — [Brown et al. 2023, PMC10354021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10354021/) (per search summary)
- PANDA: the top solution used provider-specific label-cleaning aggressiveness (20% of one provider's labels removed), implying the winners treated label quality as site-dependent. — [PANDA 1st place repo](https://github.com/kentaroy47/kaggle-panda-1st-place-solution)
- Report-image disagreements depend on context that varies by hospital/referrer/patient group (e.g. support-device patients vs ED patients), not only on the finding. — [VisualCheXbert, arXiv 2102.11467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11467)

### Inferences
- Your diagnostic (site-only AUC 0.64 against report labels vs 0.59 against expert labels) is the signature of site-dependent reporting rates; the right correction is on labels/targets, not on image features. Image-feature adversarial training (DANN/gradient reversal) removes site information from features, which also removes the real site-prevalence signal that expert labels show (0.59), and MEDFAIR/Müller say it is unreliable. I would rank it below the label-side options.
- Cheapest label-side tests, all about 1x cost: (1) site-balanced sampling or site×label-stratified batches, which is the best-evidenced of the family (Müller); (2) add a learned per-(site, label) logit offset during training and drop it at inference, which lets the network explain site-specific report prevalence without using image features (a degenerate Tanno-style confusion model; I did not find it tested on medical report labels); (3) site-specific blend weights and rejection budgets when mixing report labels with OOF predictions, as in PANDA's per-provider cleaning; (4) per-site rank/z-score standardisation of the blended soft targets before training.
- Evaluate any of these on the 58 using both pooled AUC and within-site AUC, since a site-only baseline scoring 0.59 means pooled AUC partly credits site prevalence; if within-site improves while pooled is flat, the mitigation is working but the test-set mix may still reward site cues.
- Group DRO is likely the wrong tool: it equalises worst-group loss on noisy labels, i.e. it up-weights the sites whose labels are noisiest. Not recommended without clean per-site validation, which 58 studies cannot provide.

### Gaps
- No published study tests site-specific label (not image) bias with a clean test set in a report-labelled multi-site MRI setting; the nearest are CXR dataset-merging papers (labels from the same pipeline in both domains) and PANDA (one repo, effect sizes unknown).
- MEDFAIR was read from the abstract page only; its per-method numbers and the model-selection effect were not extracted.
- I could not find the number of sites in the RSNA knee data or per-site expert counts; with 58 expert studies, per-site validation is statistically empty.

## Q5. "Uncertain"/silent report mentions: how papers encode them (U-ones, U-zeros, soft targets, smoothing), and which worked.

### Takeaway
There is no universally best encoding: on CheXpert's image-read validation set the best choice flipped by label and most differences were not significant; soft encodings (label smoothing on uncertain, self-trained probabilities, graded uncertainty-to-smoothing maps) are consistently competitive or better, giving +1.4 AUC points (Pham) and a large ablation gain on a report-derived benchmark (Rep-GLS, caveat). "Silent" mentions are different: they are one-sided noise mostly handled by treating negatives as noisy (reject/correct large-loss negatives) rather than by changing the encoding.

### Cited Findings
- CheXpert (validation = 3-radiologist image consensus, n=200; AUC, U-Ignore / U-Zeros / U-Ones / U-SelfTrained / U-MultiClass): atelectasis 0.818 / 0.811 / 0.858 / 0.833 / 0.821; cardiomegaly 0.828 / 0.840 / 0.832 / 0.831 / 0.854; consolidation 0.938 / 0.932 / 0.899 / 0.939 / 0.937; edema 0.934 / 0.929 / 0.941 / 0.935 / 0.928; effusion 0.928 / 0.931 / 0.934 / 0.932 / 0.936. U-Ones best for atelectasis and edema ("possible atelectasis" and "possible mild edema" tend to describe real findings); U-Ones worst for consolidation (0.899), explained by "atelectasis versus consolidation" mentions that the image panel usually resolved as atelectasis-positive / consolidation-negative (the paper's text says U-Zeros performs best there, whereas in my reconstructed table U-SelfTrained 0.939, U-Ignore 0.938 and U-MultiClass 0.937 edge out U-Zeros 0.932; differences among those four are under 0.01, so treat it as a tie); U-MultiClass best for cardiomegaly (borderline mentions like "minimal cardiac enlargement"). "ignoring the uncertainty label is not an effective approach". — [CheXpert, arXiv 1901.07031](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07031) (Table 3 and analysis; row order reconstructed from in-text values)
- Pham et al. replace each uncertain label with a random number in a band near 1 (U-Ones+LSR) or near 0 (U-Zeros+LSR) instead of 1/0; mean AUC on the same 200-study image-read validation set: U-Ignore 0.863, U-Zeros 0.858, U-Ones 0.860, U-Zeros+LSR 0.871, U-Ones+LSR 0.874, U-Ones+hierarchical conditional training+LSR 0.894, ensemble of six 0.940. LSR on U-Ones therefore added +1.4; per-label results were mixed (e.g. U-Zeros+LSR decreased atelectasis 0.780 → 0.745). — [Pham et al., arXiv 1911.06475](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06475) (Table 1, read from PDF text)
- Rep-GLS/LU-ViT: maps graded report uncertainty words ("likely", "unlikely", etc.) to label-smoothing rates (seven levels; high-confidence labels get negative smoothing, i.e. sharper than hard). Ablation mean AUROC: without the GLS loss 78.89, full model 84.19 (warm-up 5 epochs best). Caveat: the benchmark is built on MIMIC-CXR reports (CAD-Chest with uncertainty annotations) and I found no statement that the test labels are independent image reads, so treat this as agreement with report-derived labels, not with expert image reads; the authors list "potential overfitting to specific annotation patterns" as a limitation. — [Zhang et al., arXiv 2508.02495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02495) (Table 1, Fig. 3, read from PDF text)
- GPT-4o extraction of true/false/uncertain labels from upper-extremity radiograph reports (extraction accuracy 98.6%); re-assigning uncertain to true vs false gave no significant difference in classifier AUC (p ≥ 0.15) on internal and external tests. — [arXiv 2510.05664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05664) (abstract per page summary)
- Open-source LLM extraction for lumbar spine MRI reports (five conditions, balanced accuracy over 0.94): image classifiers trained on the automated labels "achieve comparable performance to models trained using scans manually annotated by clinicians". — [Park et al., MICCAI 2024, arXiv 2410.17235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17235) (abstract)
- Silent/omitted findings are common: sensitivity of NLP labels vs radiologist re-read of the image was 0.13–0.36 for five CXR findings (Q1); report-vs-image disagreement in head MRI is category-specific (Q1). — [Gündel et al., arXiv 2104.05261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05261); [Wood et al., PMC8660736](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8660736)
- CheXpert and later work treat unmentioned ("blank") as negative; treating unobserved labels as negative (assume-negative) then rejecting/correcting the highest-loss negatives gave +0.5–0.6 mAP over naive assume-negative on a 5000-category real partial-label dataset, with the rejection fraction growing slowly with epoch; ASL's probability shifting "can also reject mislabeled negative samples" at no extra cost. — [Large Loss Matters, arXiv 2206.03740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03740); [ASL, arXiv 2009.14119](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14119)
- A labeler trained to predict image-based labels (using a vision model as proxy supervision) beat text-faithful labelers in agreement with image reads by +0.14 F1 and gave better downstream classifiers. — [VisualCheXbert, arXiv 2102.11467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11467); [labeler-quality study, arXiv 2104.00793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00793)

### Inferences
- Because the winner flips by label and the flip pattern follows clinical wording (possible/likely findings are true findings; vague "borderline" are borderline), for an ensemble under an AUC metric the cheap hedge is encoding diversity: train a few members with different uncertain→target maps (e.g. soft LLM probability as is; probability squeezed toward the middle; probability with uncertain pushed toward 1 for labels where "possible" generally denotes a finding) and average ranks. Selecting the encoding per label on 58 studies is unreliable (Q3).
- Expert protocol here treats borderline as negative; the CheXpert-validation protocol used "uncertain likely" = positive. If the LLM probability expresses "borderline", then for this competition the right direction for the uncertain band is probably toward 0 (U-Zeros-like) for the findings where radiologists rarely call borderline cases, and toward 1 only where "possible X" typically denotes a seen finding. This is an inference from the encoding differences and the stated expert convention; it needs the 58 (aggregate over labels) to check.
- For silent mentions, treat report-negatives as noisy negatives and attack them directly: per label and epoch, reject (zero-weight) or soft-correct the top-k% highest-loss negatives, with k bounded by the estimated hidden-positive rate from the prevalence gap (about 22% of report-negatives for lateral meniscus; my arithmetic). This is almost the same mechanism as your OOF-blend but acts online and is free; expected extra gain over your existing bootstrap is likely small (+0.5 mAP-scale in the natural-image paper).
- Co-occurrence structure matters as much as the encoding: the VisualCheXbert coefficients show that a report finding raises the odds of related image findings (effusion → opacity) and that mention of one finding suppresses reporting of another. In knee MRI the same logic could apply (e.g. meniscal tear / ACL tear / effusion / cartilage), so passing all 12 report probabilities to the stacker or using label-hierarchy conditioning (Pham's conditional training gave +1.2 with LSR) may recover omitted findings.

### Gaps
- No paper tests uncertain/silent encodings against expert reads in knee MRI, and the expert convention here (borderline = negative) differs from CheXpert's (uncertain-likely = positive).
- Rep-GLS evaluates against report-derived labels; its +5.3 mAUC ablation does not establish agreement with image reads.
- The upper-extremity (GPT-4o) paper's finding (no effect of uncertainty handling) comes from an abstract-level summary; effect sizes per finding were not available.

## Q6. Synthesis: ranked, implementable techniques for ranking quality against expert reads (with compute cost)

### Takeaway
Rank by (evidence of gain on clean/image-read scoring) x (fit to a one-sided, instance-dependent noise pattern) / (T4 cost). The top of the list is cheap and mostly additions to the soft-bootstrapping you already run; the bottom (co-teaching, DivideMix, meta-learning, adversarial image-feature removal) has weak or negative evidence and 2–3x cost.

### Cited Findings
- The relevant evidence is in Q1–Q5: NVUM (+3 to +5 AUC points over plain BCE on clean CXR test sets, with DivideMix far below and ELR/co-teaching flat), Pham (ensemble +4.6, label smoothing +1.4, conditional training), VisualCheXbert (report→image mapping, +0.14 F1, learned on 500 studies), Gündel (noise priors from a re-read subset, +0.024 on consolidation), Luo (gated temporal ensembling and light adversarial site training, +1.2 total), Müller (rebalancing effective), MEDFAIR (no gain from group DRO/adversarial over ERM), LNMBench (sample-selection and semi-supervised LNL methods at or below CE on real medical noise), Large-Loss/ASL (reject or correct false-negative-type noise), Menon (AUC unchanged under class-conditional noise).

### Inferences
Ranked list (my judgement; cost = multiple of one training run):

1. Site-aware blending/targets (about 1x, trivial to try): per-site estimates in the OOF blend (site-specific mixing weight or per-site standardisation of the soft targets), and site-balanced sampling or site×label-stratified batches. Why first: your own diagnostic (0.64 vs 0.59) says site-specific label style is the dominant remaining instance-dependent noise, rebalancing is the best-evidenced site fix in imaging (Müller), PANDA's winners did provider-specific cleaning. Check with pooled and within-site AUC on the 58.
2. NVUM/ELR-style EMA self-target + class-prior logit shift added to the loss (about 1x): the only technique with multi-label clean-test evidence of a clear gain (+3 to +5 AUC points over plain BCE on OpenI/PadChest; +1 to +2 on the smaller 1,962-image NIH re-read subset; DivideMix and ELR flat or worse). Combine with the cross-architecture OOF target rather than replacing it; keep beta around 0.9 and gate the target weight by confidence (Luo's UTE).
3. Diversified ensemble (about 3–6 members at ~1x each): different architecture/seed/resolution and different uncertain→target encodings; average ranks. Best-evidenced lever in the CXR literature (Pham: best single 0.894 → ensemble of six 0.940; I did not verify whether the six differ in label encoding or architecture, so the encoding-diversity hedge is my extrapolation) and it hedges the unresolved per-label encoding question.
4. Online large-loss rejection/correction of negatives (about 1x): ASL probability-shift or LL-R/LL-Ct on report-negatives with a ramped rejection fraction capped by the prevalence-gap estimate. Targets one-sided omission noise directly; expected gain beyond your OOF blend is small.
5. Small shrunk stacker fit on the 58 (about 0x GPU, a few seconds on CPU): expert ~ own-label report logit + own-label OOF logit (+ site intercept), ridge-shrunk toward the global blend, evaluated by repeated k-fold on the 58; apply the learned weights to produce the next round's soft targets. Evidence is VisualCheXbert's report→image regression, but it used 500 studies, so keep to 2–3 parameters per label and report the CI.
6. Include the 58 expert studies in the training mix with high weight (about 1x, but destroys the validation signal): evidence from two papers that mixed training beats sequential fine-tuning; use only for a final retrain after all choices are made, with hyperparameters fixed.
7. Cheap loss tweaks (about 1x): SCE (only method that was consistently above CE on real CheXpert noise in LNMBench), label-smoothing during warm-up (MedSSL component), logit-adjusted/asymmetric BCE. Low expected gain; try only if time remains.
8. Low priority/avoid: image-feature site-adversarial training (small gain at best, unstable beyond weight 0.1, removes true site signal; about 1.2x); group DRO (up-weights noisiest sites); transition-matrix or GLC corrections (cannot change a ranking under class-conditional noise; the instance-dependent part is not captured); co-teaching/DivideMix (2x, at or below BCE on real report noise); meta-learning with clean set (2–3x, only +1.1 accuracy points in the one medical example with 200 clean items).
- Compute plan for ~60 T4-hours: items 1–4 are about 1x each and can be run as ablations inside the ensemble members rather than as separate studies; item 5 is CPU-only; do not schedule bilevel or two-network methods.

### Gaps
- All gains quoted are from chest X-ray, retina or natural-image tasks; effect sizes for knee MRI against expert reads are unknown, and the 58-study set cannot resolve effects smaller than about 0.05 macro-AUC (Q3) — the Kaggle LB is the only larger signal.
- I did not find any peer-reviewed or Kaggle write-up that trained on LLM-extracted knee-MRI report labels and scored against expert image reads; the closest MRI analogues are head MRI (Wood), lumbar spine (Park) and glioma change detection (Di Noto).
- Items 1 and 4 are my recommendations constructed from related evidence (rebalancing, PANDA provider-specific cleaning, large-loss rejection); I did not find them tested together or on report-labelled MRI.

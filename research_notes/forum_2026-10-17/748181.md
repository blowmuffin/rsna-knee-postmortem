# Kaggle could we please start doing something about the public notebooks? 
748181 | Cody_Null (@cody11null) | 2026-10-11T02:15 | votes 125

Yet again, we have another competition where low-effort public ensembles are being released with less than two weeks remaining, potentially allowing participants to secure medals through copy-paste submissions rather than their own work or understanding.

I fully expect this to continue as the deadline approaches and based on the past year or more, I expect that Kaggle will do absolutely nothing to address it.

Personally, I expect to be fine. My concern is for the participants who may end up hundreds of places below where they otherwise would have finished, despite putting in significantly more work and developing their own solutions. I am equally concerned about those who have shared genuinely valuable notebooks and helped others learn, only to see that work receive little recognition because the current code-tier structure disproportionately rewards marginal leaderboard improvements, often effectively meaningless (or outright duplicates), driven by a random seed rather than a meaningful contribution.

What I find most frustrating is not simply that this problem persists, but that it continues despite repeated forum discussions and substantial community support highlighting the issue. Why have we received so little meaningful explanation from Kaggle about why this remains unresolved, what trade-offs are preventing a solution, or whether there is any concrete plan to address it?

No system will be perfect, and I recognize that solutions involve trade-offs. But leaving participants without meaningful explanation while the same concerns recur competition after competition is difficult to justify.

Kaggle should be a place where competitive performance, original work, and meaningful contributions are rewarded. Right now, the incentives are increasingly misaligned with those goals, and I would genuinely like to understand why.

  There's also something a bit shady about the incentives behind it, whichever way you look at it:
  
  (a) If your notebook is overfitted to the public leaderboard (as often happens), you're potentially misleading other participants and sending them down the wrong path.
  
  (b) If it actually generalizes, you've effectively raised the bar overnight. People who've spent weeks developing their own solutions can suddenly find themselves pushed out of medal range by a flood of copied submissions.
  
  So whether (a) or (b) turns out to be true, some participants end up paying the price. Notebook medals are supposed to reward contributions that help the community learn, yet the current system incentivizes a practice that can come at the expense of that very same community. That seems to defeat the purpose of rewarding notebook sharing in the first place.
  
  I think this gets even worse in the newer agent competitions. If a public agent gets copied over and over, the opponent pool can become saturated with identical strategies. That can encourage people to optimize specifically against whatever happens to be overrepresented on the ladder, rather than developing agents that perform well against a wider range of opponents. Depending on the nature of the competition and how matchmaking works, this can produce some pretty misleading signals about what actually works.
  
  Of course, experienced competitors will generally know how to recognize signs of overfitting and test how well a solution generalizes. But for less experienced participants, this adds a lot of unnecessary noise and confusion.
  
  And don't get me wrong. I'm all for discussions, working notes, and people sharing experiments and what they've learned. I've personally learned far more from discussion posts that offered a few hints and opened up directions I hadn't even considered than from notebooks I could simply copy and submit. I just think the current system needs some rethinking if we want a healthier competitive environment. 
  
  Paradoxically, public notebooks might lower the barrier to entry, but end up raising the bar for newcomers trying to compete with their own work. Making it easier to copy a strong solution doesn't necessarily make a competition more accessible to those who actually want to learn and develop their own solutions. And that's something Kaggle should seriously address.

  [Po-Hao "Howard" Chen 2026-08-28T17:56] This question is best addressed at a general level rather than as a statement about how participants should interpret this specific dataset. Part of the challenge is understanding the characteristics of the training data and how the diagnostic space represented here relates to the broader complexity of musculoskeletal MRI. Any curated dataset is necessarily a subset of the real world, and even carefully defined features may not capture every combination or edge case encountered in clinical practice. Fat suppression, whether achieved by inversion recovery, frequency-selective fat saturation, or another technique, is not inherently the same as fluid sensitivity. In musculoskeletal MRI, however, the two can and often do occur together. Fluid-sensitive sequences commonly highlight edema, hemorrhage/contusion, and other abnormalities in soft tissues and bone marrow. Because subcutaneous tissues and bone marrow contain substantial amounts of fat, suppressing the fat signal generally makes these fluid-related abnormalities easier to visualize. As a result, most clinically useful fluid-sensitive MSK sequences are also fat-suppressed. That relationship is not absolute. A fluid-sensitive sequence does not necessarily have to be fat-suppressed, and a fat-suppressed sequence is not necessarily fluid-sensitive. For example, when evaluating bone tumors or musculoskeletal infection, fat-suppressed non-fluid-sensitive sequences may be used before and after contrast administration so enhancement can be assessed directly. Accordingly, while the two columns appear identical across the current training dataset, they represent conceptually distinct imaging characteristics. Their equality in the training data should not be interpreted as meaning the terms are generally interchangeable in MRI
